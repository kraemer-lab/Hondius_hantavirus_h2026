Project SOAR: The Methodology Behind the Myth

The Origin Point

Julia Mossbridge was four years old when she knew her father was going to die.

Not feared it. Not dreamed it. Knew it — with the specific, gut-seizing, pre-verbal certainty that arrives before language has words for it. A feeling she could not have explained and did not need to. He died in a car crash shortly after. She never got to hug him again.

Decades later, Mossbridge is a cognitive neuroscientist and computer scientist at Northwestern, the University of San Diego, and the Institute of Noetic Sciences — the same IONS whose founding lineage traces directly to Edgar Mitchell, the Apollo 14 astronaut who conducted unauthorized ESP experiments during his mission to the moon and returned convinced that human consciousness was something the scientific establishment had badly, dangerously misunderstood. She publishes peer-reviewed research on presentiment — the measurable physiological response to stimuli that hasn't happened yet. She studies what happens in the body and brain before the event occurs. She is, by every credential, a serious scientist pursuing a question that serious institutions have historically treated as professionally radioactive.

On May 8th of this year, Mossbridge appeared on Joe Rogan's podcast. The same day, Project SOAR dropped its first major investigative release.

Project SOAR is an ongoing investigative initiative examining whether 1980s Gifted and Talented Education programs — GATE — were used as operational cover for government-sponsored research programs targeting children with anomalously high baseline intuitive or precognitive abilities. Its methodology involves analysis of redacted school records, anomalous patterns in standardized testing results, and documented accounts of unusual screening procedures administered to children selected for gifted programs: atypical "hearing tests" with no audiological justification, pills administered without parental prescription or informed consent, psychological batteries that bore no resemblance to standard IQ or achievement assessments.

Mossbridge is Project SOAR's primary scientific lead.

Sit with that for a moment.

The leading scientific investigator into whether the U.S. government covertly screened children for psychic abilities through the public school system had her own first psychic experience — a verified precognitive event — at the age of four, triggered by the death of a parent. Her research lineage runs through IONS, through SRI International, through the same Hal Puthoff and Russell Targ remote viewing programs funded by the CIA and DIA from 1972 through 1995. The same research lineage that produced the methodology SOAR is now investigating.

This is either the most extraordinary coincidence in the history of consciousness research, or it is something else entirely.

What that something else might be requires understanding what the government has already admitted to doing — and what it burned the records to hide.

The Documented Foundation

There is a version of American history in which the CIA's MKUltra program was a costly, embarrassing failure — a Cold War fever dream that consumed millions of dollars, destroyed uncounted lives, and produced nothing of operational value. In this version, the program ended, the records were destroyed out of institutional shame, and the whole episode stands as a cautionary tale about what happens when institutions are given unchecked authority and unlimited budgets in pursuit of paranoid fantasies.

This version is the official one.

It asks you to believe several things simultaneously:

That the CIA spent twenty years and millions of dollars developing techniques of hypnotic programming, induced amnesia, personality fragmentation, and psychological manipulation — and produced nothing usable.

That CIA Director Richard Helms, in 1973, ordered the destruction of essentially all MKUltra records — not because the program had succeeded and the evidence needed to be buried, but because the program had failed and the evidence was embarrassing.

That the 20,000 pages recovered in 1977 through a Freedom of Information Act request — the fraction that survived incineration because they were misfiled in a records annex — are sufficient basis on which to evaluate the program's scope, its methods, and its results.

That none of the documented experimental achievements — verified hypnotic amnesia, verified dissociative state induction, verified post-hypnotic command execution in unwitting subjects, explicitly stated goals of creating controllable assassins — were ever applied operationally.

Each of these beliefs, held individually, requires some intellectual charity. Held simultaneously, they constitute a faith position, not an analytical one.

What the surviving documents actually establish is this:

Beginning in 1953, the CIA funded and directed experiments across at least 80 institutions — universities, hospitals, prisons, and private research facilities — into the modification of human behavior and consciousness through drugs (most famously LSD, but also mescaline, barbiturates, amphetamines, and combinations thereof), hypnosis, sensory deprivation, electroconvulsive therapy, psychological torture, and what internal documents called "terminal experiments." Subjects included prisoners, psychiatric patients, substance addicts, sex workers, and unwitting civilians — populations selected precisely because their testimony would be disbelieved if they ever managed to give it coherently.

The documented experimental goals included: the creation of durable, retrievable amnesia in healthy subjects; the implantation of false memories that subjects would defend under interrogation as genuine; the induction of hypnotic states deep enough that subjects would perform complex physical actions with no subsequent recollection; the creation of what internal documents called "multiple personality" configurations — what contemporary psychiatry calls dissociative identity disorder — in subjects who had not previously exhibited such configurations.

The explicit operational target, stated in internal memoranda that survived incineration, was the creation of an individual who could be given instructions in one psychological state and execute those instructions in another, with no conscious knowledge connecting the two states. An assassin who could not confess because he genuinely did not know. A carrier who could not be broken under interrogation because the information he carried was stored in a compartment of his own mind he could not access.

The Manchurian Candidate. Not the film. The program.

The official position is that this goal was never achieved. The destruction of the records that would prove or disprove this claim was ordered by the man who ran the organization pursuing the goal.

You are being asked to take his word for it.

The Castro Problem

Before examining the application of this methodology to American political history, it is worth establishing a baseline for what American intelligence was capable of — and, crucially, what it demonstrably was not capable of — when operating through conventional means.

Fidel Castro ruled Cuba for 49 years. He died in his bed, at 90, of natural causes, on November 25, 2016.

During his rule, Fabián Escalante — former chief of Cuban counterintelligence, the man whose professional life was dedicated to keeping Castro alive — documented an estimated 638 assassination attempts organized primarily by the CIA. Even accounting for institutional inflation of threat statistics, even discounting the more baroque and implausible schemes, the confirmed attempts are extraordinary: exploding cigars engineered to detonate in the mouth, poisoned diving suits prepared to deliver fungal spores to his skin, femme fatales recruited and trained to administer botulism toxin via beauty products, milkshakes spiked with lethal compounds, a seashell packed with explosives and placed in Castro's favorite diving location. Multiple administrations. Multiple CIA directors. The full operational and financial resources of the world's most sophisticated intelligence apparatus, applied continuously over nearly five decades.

Six hundred and thirty-eight attempts. One man. Zero successes.

Castro outlived John F. Kennedy by 53 years. He outlived Robert F. Kennedy by 48 years. He outlived every American president who tried to kill him.

Now consider what the Warren Commission asks you to accept.

A former Marine with a mail-order Mannlicher-Carcano rifle — a weapon that professional marksmen have described as neither accurate nor reliable — fired three shots in approximately 5.6 seconds from a sixth-floor window at a moving target in a vehicle traveling at 11.2 miles per hour, at a downward angle, through a tree canopy that partially obstructed the sight line, achieving two hits, one of which struck the target in the head with fatal precision. This same man had recently received a score of "rather poor" on his Marine Corps rifle qualification test. This same man had, by most witness accounts at the Dallas range where he practiced, demonstrated mediocre marksmanship. This same man's alleged shot trajectory required a bullet to change direction in mid-flight — the famous Single Bullet Theory, which the Warren Commission's own critics called, less charitably, the Magic Bullet Theory — passing through Kennedy's neck, emerging, striking Governor Connally in the back, traveling through his chest, shattering his wrist, and embedding itself in his thigh, before being recovered in near-pristine condition on a hospital gurney.

The CIA, with unlimited resources and decades of effort, could not kill a man sitting still in a fortified compound.

Lee Harvey Oswald, with a $21.45 rifle, allegedly killed the President of the United States on his first attempt.

The House Select Committee on Assassinations reviewed this evidence in 1979 and concluded that Kennedy was "probably assassinated as a result of a conspiracy." They identified acoustic evidence of a second shooter on the grassy knoll. They named no conspirators. The finding has never been resolved, revised, or retracted. It sits in the historical record like a wound that was never properly dressed: acknowledged, documented, and deliberately not examined.

The Warren Commission's conclusion was not a finding of fact. It was a political necessity. The alternative — that the President of the United States had been killed by a coordinated intelligence operation — carried implications that the government of the United States, in November 1963, was structurally incapable of surviving. The lone gunman was not selected because the evidence pointed to him. The lone gunman was selected because the alternative could not be allowed to be true.

The Legend Builders: Mexico City, 1963

Understanding how a patsy is constructed requires understanding what "legend" means in intelligence terminology.

A legend is a fabricated identity — a documented history, created in advance, that will withstand scrutiny after the fact. Legends are built through paper trails: visa applications, telephone calls, witnessed encounters, documented associations. The legend doesn't need to be real. It needs to be recorded. Documents don't lie about what they are; they lie about what they mean.

In the autumn of 1963, someone built a legend for Lee Harvey Oswald in Mexico City.

The CIA's Mexico City station, operating under chief of station Win Scott, maintained what was at the time one of the most sophisticated surveillance operations in the Western Hemisphere. The Soviet and Cuban diplomatic missions were under constant, comprehensive monitoring: every visitor photographed, every telephone call recorded, every communication logged. The station's intercept capability was, in the intelligence community's own assessment, exemplary.

Between September 27 and October 1, 1963 — seven weeks before Dallas — a series of telephone calls were placed to the Soviet and Cuban diplomatic missions by a man who identified himself as Lee Harvey Oswald. Those calls were recorded. Here is what was actually said.

September 27, 1963 — 10:37 a.m.
A man calls the Soviet Embassy. His accent is unmistakably American. His Spanish is halting, clumsy, the grammar approximate. He identifies himself and inquires about visa processing. The CIA's transcribers log the call. The voice is assigned to Lee Harvey Oswald.

September 27, 1963 — 4:05 p.m.
Silvia Duran, a Mexican national employed at the Cuban Consulate, calls the Soviet Embassy. The call is recorded:

"There is an American here who has requested an in-transit visa because he is going to Russia. I would like to know whom he spoke to at the Russian Embassy, because I sent him to you thinking if he got a Russian visa that I could then issue him a Cuban visa without any more processing. Who did he speak to? He claims he was told there were no more problems."

The Soviet official asks her to hold. Someone in the background takes the American's information. They say they'll call back. A connection between Oswald and both Soviet and Cuban diplomatic missions is now formally logged in CIA intercept records.

September 28, 1963 — 11:51 a.m.
The man calls again. This time, he speaks Russian. The transcribers struggle. Their notes describe it as "terrible, hardly recognizable Russian" — the language of a textbook reader, someone who studied the words but never inhabited the tongue.

"I was in your Embassy and I spoke to your Consul."
"What else do you want?"
"I was just now at your Embassy and they took my address."
"I know that."
"I did not know it then. I went to the Cuban Embassy to ask them for my address because they have it."
"Why don't you come by and leave it then, we're not far."
"Well, I'll be there right away."

October 1, 1963 — 11:30 a.m.
The final recorded call. The same voice. The same broken Russian. This time, explicit identification:

"Hello, this is Lee Oswald."

He asks about a telegram to Washington. He inquires about his visa. He presses: "Have they done anything?" The Soviet official — identified in surveillance logs as Obyedkov — confirms a request has been sent and hangs up mid-sentence.

Forty-nine days later, Kennedy was dead, and Lee Harvey Oswald — the American Marxist with documented contacts at Soviet and Cuban diplomatic missions, captured on CIA surveillance intercepts — was the perfect candidate for "lone communist assassin." The legend was already filed. The paper trail was already in the archives.

Here is what the official record does not comfortably accommodate:

CIA officers who reviewed the surveillance photographs of the man visiting the Soviet Embassy during this period reported that the individual in the photographs did not match Lee Harvey Oswald's physical description. He was taller, heavier, and older than the 23-year-old Oswald. The CIA's own photographic surveillance — the exemplary surveillance it operated at its most sophisticated station — had recorded someone else entering the Soviet Embassy while telephone calls to that same embassy were being logged under Oswald's name.

CIA counterintelligence chief James Angleton reviewed the Mexico City material and reportedly described it as "a provocation." He did not elaborate publicly on whose provocation it was, or against whom it was directed.

Someone was in Mexico City, using Oswald's name, establishing documented associations with Soviet and Cuban intelligence services. Someone was building a legend. Not for themselves — Oswald's name served no purpose in Mexico City for whoever was actually there. The legend was being built for Oswald. For a future moment when those intercepts would need to be pulled from the files and presented as evidence of a communist conspiracy.

The perfect patsy doesn't need to know he's a patsy. He doesn't need to understand the legend being written around him. He only needs to exist in the right place at the right time, with a rifle and a documented history of communist sympathies, and the story tells itself.

What the story doesn't tell you is who wrote it.

The Radio Man: Sirhan and the Mechanics of Programming

The Kennedy assassination has accumulated so much mythology, so many competing theories and counter-theories and counter-counter-theories, that it has become, for many people, epistemically inaccessible. There are too many variables. Too many possible explanations. The sheer volume of contested information functions as a kind of cover — the conspiracy of interpretation, which is in some ways more effective than any conspiracy of action.

The assassination of Robert F. Kennedy is cleaner. Less mythology. More mechanics. And considerably more disturbing for it.

On June 5, 1968, Sirhan Bishara Sirhan — a 24-year-old Palestinian immigrant, horse enthusiast, and Rosicrucian initiate — shot Robert F. Kennedy in the kitchen of the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles. Kennedy died the following day. Sirhan was tackled immediately by bystanders, disarmed, and arrested at the scene. There was no question about whether he fired the weapon. Ballistics and witnesses confirmed it. He was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to death, later commuted to life imprisonment.

He has been eligible for parole multiple times. Parole boards in California require, as a baseline condition for release, an acknowledgment of guilt and demonstrated remorse. Sirhan has never been released.

For over fifty years, Sirhan Sirhan has maintained a consistent, unchanging account of the night Kennedy died: he has no memory of it. Not a selective memory. Not a strategic amnesia that protects him from self-incrimination. A genuine, total, forensically investigated absence of memory for the act itself.

"I thought I was at the shooting range," he has said. "I thought I was shooting at a paper target."

This is not a denial of the act. It is something stranger and more troubling — a dissociation between the body that committed the act and the conscious mind that inhabited that body. The operational Sirhan and the aware Sirhan, in that moment and in all the moments of memory that followed, appear to have been two different people sharing one skull.

The notebooks are the first forensic anomaly. Seized immediately after the assassination, they contained pages — multiple pages — of repetitive writing: RFK must die. RFK must die. RFK must die. The phrase written over and over, sometimes for entire pages, in what handwriting analysts examining the samples described as automatic writing: the production of written language while the conscious mind is disengaged, the hand moving independently of deliberate intention, the pen controlled by something operating below the threshold of awareness. The kind of writing produced under deep hypnotic trance. The kind of writing you produce when you don't know you're writing.

Sirhan does not remember writing those pages. He does not remember having thoughts about Robert Kennedy that would explain writing those pages. He has never, in five decades of interviews, parole hearings, and psychological evaluations, produced an account of why he wanted Kennedy dead that convincingly explains the pages. His stated conscious motivations — Kennedy's support for selling fighter jets to Israel — are ideologically coherent but psychologically insufficient to explain the depth and compulsion of the notebook entries, the dissociative act itself, or the total amnesia that followed.

Now consider what investigators found when they traced Sirhan's life in the months before the assassination.

He frequented a shooting range in the San Gabriel Valley. At this range, he was befriended by an older man whose primary conversation topic was shortwave and ham radio — a shared interest that gave the two men reason to spend time together, to talk between shooting sessions, to develop a relationship that range staff and other regulars observed but did not find remarkable. This man — never identified, never charged, never named in any official proceeding — was witnessed speaking privately to Sirhan on multiple occasions, leaning close, his voice inaudible to those nearby.

Investigators called him Radio Man.

What investigators subsequently established — through a chain of connections that the official investigation declined to pursue and that independent researchers have documented in detail — was that Radio Man had an association with Dr. Louis Jolyon West.

Dr. West was not an obscure figure. He was the chairman of the Department of Psychiatry at UCLA, one of the most prominent psychiatrists in the United States, a decorated researcher, and — as documents recovered from the MKUltra files confirm — a CIA contractor whose specific area of research was hypnosis, dissociation, and the induced creation of alternate personality configurations. West studied how the human mind could be partitioned into functional compartments that operated independently of one another, with no shared memory or awareness between states. He was the CIA's leading expert on exactly the process that Sirhan Sirhan appears to have undergone.

West also, notably, conducted the psychiatric evaluation of Jack Ruby — the man who shot Lee Harvey Oswald two days after Kennedy's assassination, eliminating the only person who could have provided direct testimony about what Oswald actually knew and who he actually worked for. Ruby, too, claimed memory anomalies. Ruby, too, behaved in ways that his own stated motivations could not adequately explain. West evaluated both men and his evaluations both concluded, in different ways, that the subjects' conscious accounts of their actions were the whole story.

Chase Hughes — a behavioral analyst, former military intelligence asset, and one of the foremost working experts on covert influence and psychological manipulation, whose professional background makes him a credible and unusually specific witness — addressed the mechanics of this directly in an appearance on Joe Rogan's podcast:

"It's not about being skilled at a specific task. If you're doing something like this, it's about finding a target that is highly suggestible. If you have basic skills, I could give you three or four days of training in covert hypnosis and how to create amnesia in a person, where you can tell someone to forget something and they'll willingly forget it. I could train you in three days to do that."

"It's terrifying how our brains don't have a firewall. The moment our conscious filter — which asks, 'Is this information good for me? Should I accept this?' — is bypassed, it's very easy to just jam whatever you want into someone's head."

Hughes is describing not a theoretical vulnerability but a documented, reproducible, trainable capability. Three to four days of training. Not a decade of research. Not laboratory conditions. A long weekend and a sufficiently suggestible subject.

MKUltra's researchers spent twenty years refining exactly this capability with effectively unlimited funding and no ethical oversight.

The Methodology: What the Architecture Gives You

Understand what MKUltra actually provides, operationally, if it achieved even a fraction of its documented goals.

It gives you an instrument that cannot be traced — not because it leaves no fingerprints, but because its fingerprints belong entirely to itself. The instrument believes he acted. The instrument cannot confess to programming he has no memory of receiving. The instrument, under the most sophisticated interrogation available, will produce only the conscious narrative, which is either blank or constructed from pre-existing ideological material that was selected precisely because it provides a ready-made motive.

Oswald had already defected to the Soviet Union and returned. His communist associations were already documented by multiple intelligence agencies. His profile was already built. The Pipeline: From MKUltra to the Classroom

Here is where the through-line becomes visible — and where Project SOAR's central thesis stops being speculative and starts being the logical extension of documented practice.

MKUltra required subjects. The selection criteria were specific: high suggestibility, baseline psychological instability or prior trauma, social marginalization that reduced the likelihood of credible complaint, and — in the research lineage that eventually produced the CIA's remote viewing programs at SRI — anomalous sensory or intuitive processing. The remote viewing program, formally designated SCANATE and later STARGATE, was not looking for average perceivers. It was looking for people who demonstrated measurable precognitive or anomalous information access — abilities that the researchers documented, with increasing confidence, as real, reproducible, and inconsistently distributed across the population.

Hal Puthoff and Russell Targ at SRI spent years developing the protocols to identify such individuals. Their research, funded by the CIA and DIA, produced a methodology for screening. It also produced a problem: screening adults is inefficient. Adults have histories, connections, legal protections, and — in the case of the most psychically sensitive — the kind of developed intuition that makes deception difficult. Adults are harder to manage.

The gifted programs of the late 1970s and 1980s provide an alternative.

GATE — Gifted and Talented Education — was a federally mandated and federally funded expansion of special programming for academically exceptional children, authorized under the Gifted and Talented Children's Education Act of 1978. It created, in every participating school district in the country, a structured, institutionally sanctioned system for identifying children who processed information differently — who demonstrated anomalous pattern recognition, intuitive leaps, and cognitive profiles that diverged significantly from baseline.

Project SOAR's investigators have documented a pattern of anomalous practices in GATE programs across multiple states in the 1980s that have no legitimate educational justification. Children selected for GATE programs were administered what were described to parents as "hearing tests" that bore no resemblance to standard audiological screening — tests that, based on survivor accounts, more closely resembled the sensory sensitivity assessments used in parapsychological research. Children were administered pills by school personnel without documented prescriptions or verified parental consent. Psychological assessments included components that standard gifted education protocols do not require and cannot explain. School records for these programs contain redactions and gaps that do not appear in standard educational administrative archives.

Mossbridge, examining this material as scientific lead, is not examining it from the outside. She is examining it from a position that — if the methodology SOAR is investigating was real — she may herself have been produced by. A precognitive experience at age four, triggered by parental death: this is exactly the trauma-and-sensitivity profile that the SRI research identified as a marker for anomalous intuitive processing. The research lineage she works within — IONS, SRI's remote viewing protocols, the Puthoff/Targ framework — is exactly the lineage that the government funded to develop screening methodology.

The scientist investigating the pipeline may be a product of the pipeline. This is not an accusation. It may not even be something she fully knows. It is simply the implication of a set of facts that, held together, describe a coherent system.

And coherent systems, when they exist, do not assemble themselves by accident.

The Silence

In 1973, Richard Helms ordered the destruction of the MKUltra files.

Not some files. Not the most sensitive files. Essentially all files. The order was comprehensive. The incineration was systematic. What survived did so only because someone, somewhere, filed something in the wrong place — a bureaucratic accident that left 20,000 pages in a records annex that the destruction order didn't reach.

Those 20,000 pages tell us enough to know that what was destroyed was more important than what survived. They tell us the goals. They tell us the methods. They tell us the institutional infrastructure. They tell us, explicitly, what the program was trying to achieve.

They do not tell us whether it worked.

The absence of that information is not neutral. It is the most important data point in the entire record. Agencies destroy evidence of failure out of embarrassment. They destroy evidence of success to preserve operational capability and institutional protection. The scale, the systematicity, and the timing of the MKUltra destruction — ordered by the director of an agency under Congressional scrutiny, in the year before the Church Committee hearings that would expose the program's existence — does not describe an organization protecting itself from the revelation of an embarrassing failure.

It describes an organization protecting something that worked.

What worked. Where it was applied. Who it produced. Who it was used on. Who authorized it, managed it, and benefited from it. What became of the methodology when the formal program ended — whether "ending" the program meant stopping the research or simply relocating it, defunding it publicly while continuing it through different institutional channels, different cover programs, different funding streams. Whether the GATE programs of the 1980s were one of those channels.

These are the questions that exist in the space where the files used to be.

Richard Helms was asked, under oath, why he ordered the destruction. He said he was concerned about the program being misunderstood. About its methods being taken out of context.

He did not say the program had failed.

He said he was concerned about how it would look.

You don't burn twenty years of records to protect the reputation of something that didn't work. You burn twenty years of records because what worked is still working, and the records would tell people where to look.

Project SOAR is looking.
